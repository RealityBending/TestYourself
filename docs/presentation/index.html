Documentation

The trouble with surveys

The instruments are rarely what fails. The answering is.

Psychology runs on questionnaires, nearly all asked the same way: a page of radio buttons on a platform built for the person writing the study, not the person answering it. No validation survives careless answers.

For the participantNothing makes it worth finishing

  • Built for the researcher

    Qualtrics, Gorilla, jsPsych: good at building a study, indifferent to taking one. Grid after grid, then a thank-you.

  • Boredom and fatigue

    The same item is answered worse at the end of a long battery, and those who stop halfway are not a random half.

  • Nothing back

    An hour of honest answers buys a debriefing sheet: no reason to care how you answer, come back or tell anyone.

For the dataSo what comes back is weak

  • Careless responding

    Often a tenth of an online sample or more (Meade & Craig, 2012), and enough to make unrelated scales correlate (Huang et al., 2015).

  • Jingle and jangle

    Most scales are used once or twice (Elson et al., 2023) and seldom side by side, so whether grit is just conscientiousness is rarely checked.

  • Small, closed datasets

    A few scales, a few hundred people, a file kept on a drive. Pooling them later means reconciling what never matched.

What the Abyss is

A survey people want to finish.

The questionnaires are ordinary validated ones, asked as written. Everything around them is designed for the person answering, so that the last question is read as carefully as the first.

The aim: high-quality, re-usable, open datasets, with many measures answered by the same people, so how far they overlap can be seen.

For the participantReasons to keep going

  • Staged progress

    Levels, dressed as a dive: a depth gauge, landmarks passing, the seabed and the rock below.

  • Feedback

    Each level closes on a figure drawn from the answers just given: a body, a hill, a sea, a wheel.

  • Agency

    After the first level, participants pick which part of themselves to test next, from two or three cards.

  • Collection

    Each level mints a badge onto a shelf, and a whole-run profile fills in as the levels do.

For the dataAnswers worth keeping

  • Asking back

    Every reading can be agreed or disagreed with, every level rated: the Barnum effect, caught in the act.

  • Quiet quality control

    Attention checks, response times and an honesty question, recorded for every level and shown to nobody.

  • Nothing lost

    Answers are saved as they are given, and a run left partway picks up where it stopped.

  • Sharing

    Results go out as a link or an image that opens into the test: recruitment by word of mouth, filed as such.

Built to be changed: the questions are data rather than code, and each project asks its own levels from its own link.

MINT

Multidimensional Interoceptive Traits questionnaire · Makowski et al.

Where it comes from

Its first validation (ER/MB2021/2, two studies) established its structure and how it relates to other interoceptive scales. With the two later studies that asked it too, 1,684 people are its norms.

In this study

  1. Replication of structure on a new, larger sample with the same design, and its convergent and discriminant validity against the measures around it.
  2. Effect of response format. Each run is dealt one of three formats at random, all scored 0 to 6, to see whether the numbers on a scale change how it is answered.
  3. Correlates with HiTOP, the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology, which describes mental ill health as dimensions rather than diagnoses: to assess the relationship between interoception and psychopathology within that framing.

BAIT

Beliefs about Artificial Intelligence Technology · Makowski et al.

Where it started

As a trait measure that could partly modulate and mediate the anti-AI bias the lab measures in its tasks, drawing on two things:

Expectations about what AI can produce Current AI algorithms can generate very realistic videos
Attitudes, items carried over from the GAAIS Much of society will benefit from a future full of AI

partly modulates and mediates

Anti-AI bias in the lab's tasks: the same image judged less favourably once it is believed to be made by AI

Where it is going

Its psychometric properties have not been ideal, and more work is needed to make it a better measure.

  1. Sharper expectations. Improve the sensitivity of what it measures about what AI can produce, across modalities: images, text, art and more.
  2. Subjective discriminability. Explore a separate factor for how well people think they can tell AI's work from a person's. Four such items are asked already, and not yet scored.
  3. Usage, taken further. Extend it to AI addiction and "AI psychosis": the bond with a chatbot and the spiral the reports describe.

HiTOP

Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology · Kotov et al., 2017

What it is

A classification of mental ill health built from how symptoms go together rather than from diagnostic categories. Problems sit on spectra that everybody falls somewhere along, arranged in a hierarchy from one general factor down to single symptoms.

The short measure

The HiTOP-BR (Brief Report; Simms et al., 2026): 45 statements about the last twelve months, on four points from "Not at all" to "A lot", none reversed, scored as the mean of each of six spectra. Its norms are the development sample's (N = 780), through the {hitop} R package, which also scores a saved file directly.
